At the heart of scientific imaging lies an inverse problem of inferring latent structure from indirect observations. Remarkably, the relationship between a physical system and its measurements is often governed by rich geometric structures and symmetries.

E(3)\mathrm{E}(3)

The X-ray transform underlying computed tomography intertwines distinct representations of the Euclidean group (Helgason 1999).

SO(3)\mathrm{SO}(3)

In cryo-electron microscopy, molecular structure must be recovered from projections acquired at unknown orientations (Bendory et al. 2020).

SO(2)\mathrm{SO}(2)

In cosmic microwave background polarimetry, linear polarization transforms according to a spin-2 representation of rotations of the reference frame (Zaldarriaga and Seljak 1997).

Geometry thus enters scientific imaging through forward operators, spaces of unknowns, and the transformation laws of measured quantities. My research seeks to derive computational methods from the intrinsic mathematical structure of scientific problems. I am interested in how the physics of observation shapes signal spaces, how physical quantities transform and evolve, and how geometry informs operators for learning and inference, drawing on differential geometry, representation theory, inverse problems, and machine learning.

Much of this research originated in diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (dMRI), the principal application of my doctoral work. Its acquisition geometry couples spatial and directional information through a common reference frame.

Geometry induced by acquisition

The anatomical structures that diffusion MRI seeks to reveal are often orders of magnitude smaller than the spatial resolution of the scanner. What makes their investigation possible is that microscopic tissue architecture leaves an imprint on the motion of water molecules. Within a coherently organized bundle of nerve fibers, for instance, water tends to diffuse more readily along the fibers than across them (Basser et al. 1994). More generally, the statistics of molecular displacement provide an indirect window into tissue organization at otherwise inaccessible scales (Figure 1).

The scanner probes these statistics by applying magnetic field gradients that encode molecular displacement into the phase of the magnetic resonance signal. Under the narrow-pulse approximation, the normalized complex signal at a fixed diffusion time is the Fourier transform of the voxel-averaged probability density of molecular displacement (Callaghan 1991), with the wavevector as the frequency variable. A diffusion MRI acquisition therefore produces a family of images indexed by wavevector.

The signal thus depends jointly on spatial position 𝒑\mathbf{p} and wavevector 𝒒\mathbf{q}, giving rise to the ambient acquisition space ℝ3×ℝ3\mathbb{R}^3 \times \mathbb{R}^3. In multi-shell acquisitions, nonzero wavevectors are sampled from concentric spheres of fixed radii, supplemented by measurements at zero wavevector. Spatial position and wavevector are expressed relative to a common reference frame. A rigid motion (R,t)∈SE(3)(R,t)\in\mathrm{SE}(3) induces the joint action

(𝒑,𝒒)↦(R𝒑+t,R𝒒).(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}) \longmapsto (R\mathbf{p}+t,\,R\mathbf{q}).

Although the acquisition space is a Cartesian product, this action couples its spatial and wavevector coordinates.

From symmetry to convolution

In my early work on neurodegenerative disorder classification, I developed a convolutional neural network (CNN) that operated directly on measured diffusion signals through successive spatial and angular convolutions (Cortés et al. 2024). This separation, however, did not guarantee equivariance to rigid motions of the acquisition space, meaning that a transformation of the input need not induce the corresponding transformation of the output. Inspired by the work of Cohen et al. (2019), I shifted my focus from designing individual spatio-angular architectures to understanding the class of convolutional operators compatible with the underlying symmetry.

I subsequently developed a unified geometric framework for classifying existing spatio-angular CNNs (Müller et al. 2021; Lyon et al. 2023; Bekkers et al. 2024; Elaldi et al. 2024a, 2024b; Liu et al. 2025; Hussain and Khan 2025). I evaluated these architectures against two symmetry requirements arising from the acquisition geometry: equivariance to the joint action of rigid motions and compatibility with the antipodal symmetry of the signal magnitude under wavevector reversal, 𝒒↦−𝒒\mathbf{q}\mapsto-\mathbf{q}. Guided by these requirements, I proposed constructing spatio-angular CNNs directly from 3D steerable convolutions (Weiler et al. 2018), drawing on their lesser-known equivalence to rigid-motion-equivariant spatio-angular convolutions (Bekkers et al. 2024, Appendix A.2). In experiments with rigid motions preserving the voxel grid, the resulting implementation achieved an equivariance error two orders of magnitude lower than that of the next-best baseline.

Beyond rigid motions

The joint action of rigid motions raises a more general question of how diffusion MRI signals should transform under nonrigid deformations. This question is central to image registration, where the goal is to establish anatomical correspondence between individuals. A deformation of physical space specifies where each voxel moves, but does not by itself specify how its associated wavevectors should transform. The problem is therefore as much one of defining an appropriate notion of deformation as of estimating one.

An arbitrary deformation of the six-dimensional acquisition space could map measurements acquired at a single voxel to different spatial locations, depending on their wavevectors (Figure 2). This motivated me to model the acquisition space as a vector bundle over physical space (Cortés et al. 2026), restricting admissible deformations to those that preserve its bundle structure. Within this framework, I developed a reorientation strategy that preserves the sampled wavevector magnitudes and extends the classical finite-strain rule (Alexander et al. 2001) from diffusion tensors to the measured signal itself.

To estimate these deformations, I developed a steerable registration network based on VoxelMorph (Balakrishnan et al. 2019). To our knowledge, it is the first equivariant registration network operating directly on raw diffusion MRI measurements in their native acquisition space (Cortés et al. 2026). By construction, a common rigid motion of the input images induces the corresponding transformation of the predicted deformation, up to discretization error. Using the same optimizer, registration of the measured diffusion signal yielded an endpoint error of 0.15 voxels on known smooth deformations of real data, compared with 0.38 voxels for fractional anisotropy, a scalar summary of diffusion. On held-out subjects from two datasets, one acquired on a different scanner, our method achieved higher mean Dice overlap of anatomical structures than the classical and learned baselines evaluated. On inputs in their original orientation, the steerable network matched a conventional U-Net’s registration accuracy with 58% of its parameters.

Revisiting the geometry of measurement

In my registration work, I preserved wavevector magnitudes to accommodate multi-shell sampling, not because their intrinsic transformation law requires it. A diffusion-encoding wavevector pairs with a molecular displacement to determine a phase difference, a quantity that must remain invariant under affine changes of coordinates. Wavevectors therefore transform as covectors rather than vectors, a distinction concealed by rigid motions but revealed by a shear (Figure 3). The physics of measurement thus identifies the ambient acquisition space not merely as a vector bundle over physical space, but specifically as its cotangent bundle.

The general vector bundle formulation leaves a choice of fiberwise transformation, whereas the canonical cotangent lift determines it from the spatial transformation alone. Whether this canonical law for changes of coordinates should also govern anatomical deformation remains an open question.

Beyond equivariance

Equivariance alone does not determine the geometric relationships an operator can express. In spherical deconvolution, the inverse problem of recovering fiber orientation distributions from diffusion measurements, I am investigating whether steering improves inference by allowing spatial evidence to be gathered preferentially along candidate fiber directions.

Toward geometric foundations of scientific computation

Geometry of inference. I seek to understand how symmetries of physical systems are inherited or broken by acquisition, and how the geometry of forward operators can inform reconstruction and inference from incomplete measurements.

Geometry beyond equivariance. I am interested in what geometric relationships operators respecting a common symmetry can express, and how their mathematical structure shapes trade-offs between approximation accuracy, data requirements, and computational cost.

Geometry of evolution. Beyond imaging, I want to explore how the geometry of a system’s state space and its governing physical laws can guide computational models of its evolution.

I am particularly drawn to problems where mathematics and computation can advance scientific understanding or contribute to human health.
